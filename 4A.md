## 1. What the PS asks (in plain words)
Classify movie reviews as positive or negative using **CBOW word embeddings**: prepare and tokenise the text, train Word2Vec with CBOW, turn each review into a vector, train a feed-forward classifier on those vectors and evaluate it.

## 2. Dataset
- `IMDB Dataset.csv`: **50,000 reviews** (`review` text, `sentiment` positive/negative), balanced. The text contains HTML line breaks (`<br />`).
- Your split: 80/20, so 40,000 training and 10,000 test reviews (Word2Vec is trained on the 40,000 only). The vectors have 100 numbers each.

## 3. Notebook walkthrough: every cell, and why

### Cell 0: imports
```python
import tensorflow as tf
from tensorflow import keras
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from gensim.models import Word2Vec
from gensim.utils import simple_preprocess
from gensim.parsing.preprocessing import STOPWORDS
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.metrics import classification_report
```
**Why:** `tensorflow`/`keras` for the classifier; `gensim` for `Word2Vec`; `simple_preprocess` to tokenise; `STOPWORDS` is gensim's stop-word list; `train_test_split` and `classification_report` from scikit-learn; `matplotlib` for the curves.

### Cell 1: load the reviews
```python
d = pd.read_csv("IMDB Dataset.csv")
d.head()
```
**Why:** Reads the CSV; `d.head()` shows the `review` and `sentiment` columns.

### Cell 2: stop-word list that keeps negations
```python
STOP = STOPWORDS - {"not","no","nor","never"}                # gensim list, but keep the negations (they flip sentiment)
t = "this movie was not good and never funny"
print([w for w in simple_preprocess(t) if w not in STOP])      # ours: negations kept
print([w for w in simple_preprocess(t) if w not in STOPWORDS]) # plain gensim: negations lost
```
**Why:** `STOP = STOPWORDS - {"not","no","nor","never"}` is gensim's list with the four negations **removed from it**, so they are kept in the text. The demo shows the effect: with our list `"this movie was not good and never funny"` stays `['movie', 'not', 'good', 'never', 'funny']`; with plain gensim it becomes `['movie', 'good', 'funny']`, which sounds positive.

**Why not the alternative:** Not plain `remove_stopwords`: it deletes 'not' and 'never' and flips the sentiment. Not keeping every stop word: the very common words add noise.

### Cell 3: tokenise and build labels
```python
tok = [[w for w in simple_preprocess(t.replace("<br />"," ")) if w not in STOP] for t in d.review]
y = (d.sentiment == "positive").astype(int).values
```
**Why:** `simple_preprocess` lowercases and splits into words (so gensim's lowercase stop-word list matches); `t.replace("<br />", " ")` removes the HTML breaks first; the list comprehension drops stop words. `y` is 1 for positive and 0 for negative.

**Why not the alternative:** Not leaving the HTML (`br` would become a frequent 'word'). Not removing stop words before lowercasing (the list is lowercase).

### Cell 4: split before training Word2Vec
```python
tk_tr, tk_te, ytr, yte = train_test_split(tok,y,test_size=0.2,random_state=42)     # split BEFORE training Word2Vec
```
**Why:** The reviews are split into training and test **before** Word2Vec is trained, so the test reviews never influence the embeddings.

**Why not the alternative:** Not training Word2Vec on all 50,000 reviews first (data leakage).

### Cell 5: train CBOW
```python
w2v = Word2Vec(tk_tr,vector_size=100,window=5,min_count=5,sg=0,epochs=5)           # sg=0 -> CBOW
print(w2v.wv.most_similar("great",topn=5))
```
**Why:** `Word2Vec(tk_tr, vector_size=100, window=5, min_count=5, sg=0, epochs=5)`: 100-number vectors, 5 words of context each side, ignore words seen fewer than 5 times, **`sg=0` = CBOW**, 5 passes. `most_similar("great")` is a sanity check: excellent 0.77, wonderful 0.75, fantastic 0.73, fine 0.72, good 0.71.

**Why not the alternative:** Not skip-gram (`sg=1`): slower; CBOW is fast and fine for frequent words. Not `min_count=1`: rare words get poor vectors.

### Cell 6: document vectors
```python
vec = lambda ws: np.mean([w2v.wv[w] for w in ws if w in w2v.wv] or [np.zeros(100)],axis=0)   # document = average of its word vectors
Xtr, Xte = np.array([vec(t) for t in tk_tr]), np.array([vec(t) for t in tk_te])
print(Xtr.shape, Xte.shape)
```
**Why:** `vec` averages the vectors of the words of a review (only words that are in the vocabulary; an empty review gets a zero vector). Result: `(40000, 100)` and `(10000, 100)`.

**Why not the alternative:** Not a bag-of-words count vector: it is huge and ignores meaning. The average loses word order (a known limitation).

### Cell 7: classifier model
```python
layers = tf.keras.layers
model = keras.Sequential([layers.Input(shape=(100,)), layers.Dense(64,activation="relu"), layers.Dropout(0.3), layers.Dense(1,activation="sigmoid")])
model.summary()
```
**Why:** `Dense(64, relu)` -> `Dropout(0.3)` -> `Dense(1, sigmoid)` on the 100-number input: 6,529 parameters. Sigmoid gives P(positive).

**Why not the alternative:** Not softmax with 2 units (it also works but sigmoid is the usual binary choice). Not a big network: 100 inputs need little capacity.

### Cell 8: compile and train
```python
model.compile(optimizer="adam",loss="binary_crossentropy",metrics=["accuracy"])
hist = model.fit(Xtr,ytr,epochs=15,batch_size=64,validation_split=0.1)
```
**Why:** Adam, `binary_crossentropy` (two classes with sigmoid), 15 epochs, batch 64, `validation_split=0.1` of the training vectors.

**Why not the alternative:** Not `sparse_categorical_crossentropy`: that is for several classes with softmax.

### Cell 9: evaluate
```python
#evaluate
print(model.evaluate(Xte,yte))
pred = (model.predict(Xte) > 0.5).astype(int).ravel()                  # sigmoid output -> threshold 0.5
print(classification_report(yte,pred,target_names=["negative","positive"],digits=3))
```
**Why:** `model.evaluate` gives the test loss and accuracy; predictions are thresholded at 0.5 (`> 0.5`) and `.ravel()` flattens them for `classification_report`.

**Why not the alternative:** Not `argmax`: there is one sigmoid output, not a probability per class.

### Cell 10: training curves
```python
#plot
pd.DataFrame(hist.history).plot()
plt.show()
```
**Why:** Loss and accuracy of training against validation.

**Why not the alternative:** Not only the final accuracy.

### Cell 11: one example
```python
#one example
for k in (0,1):
    i = np.where((yte == k) & (pred == k))[0][0]                      # first review of class k that the model got right
    print(" ".join(tk_te[i][:12]), "... | true:", ["negative","positive"][k], "| P(positive) =", round(float(model.predict(Xte[i:i+1])[0,0]),3))
```
**Why:** Takes the first correctly classified negative and positive reviews and prints their first words with P(positive). The positive example starts with 'not', which survived because negations are kept.

**Why not the alternative:** Not only the aggregate accuracy.

## 4. Terminology used in this notebook

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Word embedding** | A dense vector (for example 100 numbers) for a word; words used in similar contexts get similar vectors. |
| **Word2Vec** | An algorithm (Mikolov, 2013) that learns word embeddings from a prediction task on text; gensim implements it. |
| **CBOW** | Continuous Bag of Words (`sg=0`): the averaged context words predict the centre word. Fast, good for frequent words. |
| **Skip-gram** | `sg=1`: the centre word predicts each context word. Slower, better for rare words. |
| **window (Word2Vec)** | How many words on each side count as context (5 for tweets, 3 for the tiny document). |
| **vector_size** | The length of each word vector (100 or 50). |
| **min_count** | Words rarer than this are ignored (2 for tweets, 5 for IMDB, 1 for the tiny document). |
| **Stop words** | Very common words ('the', 'and') that carry little meaning and are removed. |
| **Tokenisation** | Splitting text into words (`simple_preprocess` also lowercases and drops punctuation). |
| **Vocabulary** | The set of distinct words that received a vector. |
| **Document vector** | The average of the word vectors of a document. |
| **Sigmoid** | Activation that squashes a number to 0..1 (a probability for two classes, or a scaled value). |
| **Binary cross-entropy** | Two-class loss used with a single sigmoid output. |
| **Dropout** | Randomly switches off a fraction of units during training only, so the network cannot depend on single units (regularisation). |
| **Adam** | An optimiser that keeps running averages of the gradient and its square, so every weight gets its own adaptive step. Default learning rate 1e-3. |
| **Accuracy** | Correct predictions divided by all predictions. Misleading when classes are imbalanced. |
| **Precision** | Of everything flagged positive, the fraction that really is positive: TP / (TP + FP). |
| **Recall** | Of all real positives, the fraction found: TP / (TP + FN). |
| **F1 score** | Harmonic mean of precision and recall: 2PR / (P + R). |
| **Data leakage** | Information from test data (or from the answer) leaking into training, for example fitting a scaler on all data or keeping RUL as an input. |

## 5. Theory you must be able to explain

**Pipeline.** Text -> tokens -> Word2Vec (CBOW) word vectors -> average per review -> small neural network -> probability of positive.

**CBOW.** The words around a position (the context) are looked up in the embedding matrix and averaged; a softmax layer predicts the centre word. The rows of the embedding matrix are the word vectors. Skip-gram does the reverse and is better for rare words.

**Why embeddings.** One-hot vectors are huge and say nothing about similarity; embeddings are short and similar words (great, excellent) get similar vectors.

**Why keep negations.** 'not good' must stay negative; removing 'not' flips the meaning. Lowercase before removing stop words, because gensim's list is lowercase.

**Limitations of averaging.** Word order and negation structure are lost; better options are TF-IDF weighting, Doc2Vec or sentence encoders such as BERT.

**Evaluation.** Embeddings are judged **intrinsically** (nearest words) and **extrinsically** (the accuracy of this classifier).

## 6. Why we did this, and why not that

| Decision | Why we did it | Why not the alternative |
|---|---|---|
| Split before Word2Vec | No test reviews in the embeddings | Training Word2Vec on everything leaks test text |
| Keep negations | They flip sentiment | Plain gensim stop-word removal deletes 'not' |
| CBOW (`sg=0`), `min_count=5` | Fast; poor vectors for rare words are dropped | Skip-gram is slower; `min_count=1` keeps noisy words |
| Average word vectors | Simple, fast document vector | Loses order; TF-IDF weighting or Doc2Vec are alternatives |
| Sigmoid + binary cross-entropy | Two classes, one probability | Softmax with categorical loss is for several classes |
| Dropout 0.3 | Mild regularisation on a small head | Not needed more heavily: no strong over-fitting |

## 9. Viva questions with answers

**Q. What is CBOW?**

A. A Word2Vec model that predicts the centre word from the averaged vectors of its context words.

**Q. CBOW vs skip-gram?**

A. CBOW is faster and good for frequent words; skip-gram predicts the context from the centre word and is better for rare words.

**Q. How do you get a document vector?**

A. Average the vectors of its words.

**Q. Why keep 'not' when removing stop words?**

A. Negation flips sentiment: 'not good' must not become 'good'.

**Q. Why split before training Word2Vec?**

A. To avoid leaking test reviews into the embeddings.

**Q. Why sigmoid and binary cross-entropy?**

A. There are two classes and one output probability.

**Q. Limitation of averaging?**

A. It loses word order and negation structure.

**Q. How do you evaluate the embeddings?**

A. Intrinsically with nearest words and extrinsically with the classifier's accuracy (86 %).
